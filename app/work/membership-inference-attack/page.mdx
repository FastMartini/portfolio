        <p className={styles.sectionNumber}>01 · Context</p>
        <h2 id="problem-title">The problem</h2>
      </div>
      <div className={styles.prose}>
        <p className={styles.lead}>A classifier can keep its training reviews hidden and still reveal clues about which reviews it has seen.</p>
        <p>Membership inference predicts whether a particular sample was used to train a model. It does not need to recover the original text: knowing that a record was included can itself reveal sensitive information. Our question was whether the behavior of a sentiment classifier exposed enough signal to distinguish training reviews from unseen reviews.</p>
        <p>This was a classical machine-learning experiment: TF-IDF represented the text, and logistic regression classified sentiment. A second logistic-regression model learned to predict membership from the target classifier’s outputs.</p>

          <span>Dataset</span><h3>Two distinct IMDB subsets</h3>
          <p>We sampled 500 members from IMDB’s official training split and 500 non-members from its official test split. Sampling was stratified by sentiment with random seed 42; the target trained only on members.</p>
        </article>
        <article>
          <span>Target model</span><h3>Words to sentiment</h3>
          <p>TF-IDF used up to 5,000 terms and removed English stopwords. Its vocabulary was fitted only on member reviews, then applied to unseen reviews. Logistic regression predicted positive or negative sentiment.</p>
        </article>
        <article>
          <span>Baseline attack</span><h3>A single confidence threshold</h3>
          <p>The midpoint of member and non-member mean maximum confidence defined the threshold. Reviews at or above it were predicted to be members. The baseline was evaluated on all 1,000 reviews used to derive that threshold.</p>
        </article>
        <article>
          <span>Learned attack</span><h3>A separate membership classifier</h3>
          <p>The 1,000 attack-feature rows were split 70/30, stratified by membership with seed 42: 700 for attack training and 300 for evaluation. The held-out set contained 150 members and 150 non-members of the target model.</p>
        </article>

        <h2 id="limitations-title">Limitations</h2>
      </div>
      <div className={styles.limitationsGrid}>
        <p><strong>A controlled sample.</strong> The results cover 500 member and 500 non-member IMDB reviews, one target classifier, and one recorded run. They do not establish a general privacy guarantee or population-wide attack rate.</p>
        <p><strong>Attacker access matters.</strong> The learned attack assumes probability outputs, known sentiment labels, and labeled membership training examples. A system that exposes less information presents a different attack setting.</p>
        <p><strong>Different evaluation procedures.</strong> The baseline uses the same rows to derive and evaluate its threshold; the learned attack holds out 30%. A shared untouched evaluation set would support a stronger comparison.</p>
        <p><strong>No uncertainty estimate.</strong> The report does not provide repeated runs, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests. Small differences between methods should be interpreted cautiously.</p>
        <p><strong>No mitigation evaluation.</strong> Differential privacy is discussed as related work but was not implemented or tested. This experiment measures leakage rather than establishing a defense.</p>
        <p><strong>Scope of the evidence.</strong> The study uses classical text classification and public movie reviews. It neither extracts private text nor establishes how these findings transfer to other datasets or model families.</p>
